# Week 1 — Stock Market Fundamentals

**Time:** 2–3 hours · **Assumes:** zero prior knowledge
**Worksheet:** [Nifty 50 exploration](../exercises/01-nifty50-exploration.md)

---

## 1. Learning objectives

By the end of this week you should be able to:

1. Explain in one sentence what owning a share actually means.
2. Describe who the players are: company, exchange, broker, depository, regulator.
3. Explain the difference between the **primary** and **secondary** market.
4. Explain what Nifty 50 and Sensex measure — and what they do *not* measure.
5. Place a hypothetical order correctly using the right order type.
6. Read a stock quote page: LTP, bid, ask, volume, market cap, 52-week range.

## 2. Concepts, explained simply

### 2.1 Share / stock

> **Share** → A unit of ownership in a company. → If a company has 100 shares and you own 1,
> you own 1% of it. → It matters because a share is *part-ownership of a business*, not a
> lottery ticket with a ticker symbol.

**Analogy:** A pizza cut into 100 slices. Buying a share = buying one slice. If the pizza gets
bigger (the business grows), your slice gets bigger too. The pizza does not grow because
people on the internet are excited about it.

Being a shareholder gives you:
- a claim on **residual profits** (dividends, if declared),
- **voting rights** on certain resolutions,
- a claim on what's left if the company is wound up (last in line, after lenders).

### 2.2 Why companies list, and where

> **Stock exchange** → An organised, regulated marketplace where shares are bought and sold.
> → In India the two main ones are **NSE** (National Stock Exchange) and **BSE** (Bombay Stock
> Exchange). → It matters because the exchange guarantees you a counterparty, a fair price
> discovery mechanism, and settlement — you're not trusting a stranger.

- **BSE** — Asia's oldest stock exchange (est. 1875). Benchmark index: **SENSEX** (30 companies).
- **NSE** — Started in 1994, fully electronic. Benchmark index: **NIFTY 50** (50 companies).

Most large companies are listed on **both**. Prices on the two are nearly identical because
arbitrageurs instantly trade away any gap.

### 2.3 Index: Nifty 50 and Sensex

> **Index** → A basket of selected stocks whose combined value is tracked as a single number.
> → Nifty 50 = 50 large, liquid companies across sectors, weighted by free-float market cap.
> → It matters because it's the market's "thermometer": "Nifty is up 1%" is shorthand for
> "large Indian companies collectively gained about 1% today."

**What an index is NOT:** it is not the whole market. Nifty 50 excludes hundreds of mid- and
small-cap companies. "The market is up" can be true while most stocks are down.

**Free float** → Only shares actually available for public trading are counted; promoter
holdings locked away are excluded. → If a promoter owns 60%, only 40% counts toward the
index weight. → It matters because it stops closely-held companies from distorting the index.

### 2.4 Primary vs secondary market

| | Primary market | Secondary market |
|---|---|---|
| Who sells | The company (or exiting shareholders) | One investor to another |
| Money goes to | The company | The selling investor |
| Example | **IPO**, FPO, rights issue | Normal daily trading on NSE/BSE |

> **IPO (Initial Public Offering)** → The first time a company sells shares to the public and
> gets listed. → A private company raises ₹X crore and its shares begin trading on NSE/BSE.
> → It matters because it's the *only* time your money goes to the company itself; after
> listing, you're just buying from another investor.

⚠️ IPOs feel exciting and are heavily marketed. Listing-day pops are **not** a strategy. Treat
IPOs as the hardest kind of company to analyse — there's little public history.

### 2.5 The plumbing: who does what

| Entity | Role | Analogy |
|---|---|---|
| **SEBI** | Regulator of the securities market | The referee and rule-maker |
| **NSE / BSE** | The exchanges — matching engine, price discovery | The marketplace |
| **Clearing corporation** (NSE Clearing, ICCL) | Guarantees settlement of every trade | The escrow agent |
| **Broker** | SEBI-registered member who routes your order to the exchange | Your gateway / travel agent |
| **Depository — NSDL & CDSL** | Hold your shares in electronic form | The bank vault for shares |
| **Depository Participant (DP)** | Your interface to the depository (usually your broker) | The bank branch |
| **RTA** (Registrar & Transfer Agent) | Maintains shareholder records for companies | The record-keeper |

> **Demat account** → An account that holds your shares electronically. → Like a bank account,
> but it stores shares instead of rupees. → It matters because since dematerialisation,
> physical share certificates are effectively obsolete.

> **Trading account** → The account through which you *place orders*. → Bank account (money) →
> trading account (orders) → demat account (shares). → It matters because people confuse the
> two; the trading account transacts, the demat account stores.

### 2.6 How an order actually works

> **Bid** → The highest price a buyer is currently willing to pay.
> **Ask / Offer** → The lowest price a seller is currently willing to accept.
> **Spread** → Ask minus Bid.
> → Bid ₹100.00 / Ask ₹100.05 → spread is 5 paise.
> → It matters because a wide spread is a hidden cost and a sign of poor liquidity.

**Order types you must know:**

| Order type | What it does | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Market order** | Buy/sell immediately at the best available price | You don't control the price — dangerous in illiquid stocks |
| **Limit order** | Buy/sell only at your specified price or better | May never execute |
| **Stop-loss order** | A resting order that triggers once price crosses a level | Doesn't guarantee your exact price in a gap |

**Rule of thumb for a beginner:** use **limit orders**. A market order in a thinly-traded stock
can fill far away from the last price you saw.

### 2.7 Market cap, liquidity, volume

> **Market capitalisation** → Share price × total number of shares. → ₹500 × 10 crore shares =
> ₹5,000 crore market cap. → It matters because it's the *size* of the company; a ₹2,000 share
> is not "expensive" and a ₹20 share is not "cheap" until you know the share count.

SEBI's classification by market cap rank (used for mutual fund categorisation):
- **Large cap** — companies ranked 1–100 by market cap
- **Mid cap** — ranked 101–250
- **Small cap** — ranked 251 onwards

> **Volume** → Number of shares traded in a period. → 20 lakh shares traded today.
> → It matters because volume is the evidence behind a price move.

> **Liquidity** → How easily you can buy/sell a meaningful quantity without moving the price.
> → Reliance is highly liquid; a tiny small-cap may not be. → It matters because in an illiquid
> stock you can get *trapped* — there's no one on the other side when you want out.

### 2.8 Bull and bear markets

> **Bull market** → A sustained period of rising prices and optimism. (A bull thrusts its horns
> *upward*.)
> **Bear market** → A sustained decline, commonly defined as a fall of 20%+ from the peak.
> (A bear swipes its paw *downward*.)
> → It matters because your emotional state changes with the regime, and most beginner damage
> happens at the extremes of both.

**Correction** → A fall of roughly 10% from a recent high. Normal and frequent.

### 2.9 Settlement and corporate actions

> **Settlement** → The process of actually exchanging shares for money after a trade.
> → India moved to a **T+1** settlement cycle (trade day + 1 working day) for equities, ahead of
> most global markets; an optional shorter cycle has been introduced in phases.
> → It matters because it determines when shares hit your demat account and when funds are
> usable. **Verify the current cycle on the NSE/SEBI site — this rule has changed recently and may change again.**

**Corporate actions** — things a company does that affect its shares:

| Action | What happens | Effect on your holding |
|---|---|---|
| **Dividend** | Company pays out cash per share | You receive cash; price typically drops by roughly that amount on the ex-date |
| **Bonus issue** | Free additional shares (e.g. 1:1) | Share count up, price down proportionally — **your total value is unchanged** |
| **Stock split** | Face value split (e.g. ₹10 → ₹1) | More shares, lower price — **total value unchanged** |
| **Rights issue** | Existing holders offered new shares at a set price | Optional; you can subscribe or let it lapse |
| **Buyback** | Company repurchases its own shares | Share count reduces |

> **Record date / ex-date** → The cut-off that decides who is eligible. → To receive a dividend
> you must own the share *before* the ex-date. → It matters because buying "for the dividend"
> on the ex-date gets you nothing — the price has already adjusted.

⚠️ A bonus or split does **not** make you richer. It's cutting the same pizza into more slices.

## 4. Practical exercise — "Meet the Nifty 50"

Use the worksheet: [Nifty 50 exploration](../exercises/01-nifty50-exploration.md)

1. Open the Nifty 50 constituent list on niftyindices.com. Note down:
   - How many sectors are represented?
   - Which 5 companies have the **largest index weight**?
   - What % of the index do those top 5 make up? (This tells you how concentrated the index is.)
2. Pick **three** well-known companies from three *different* sectors (e.g. one bank, one IT
   services firm, one FMCG firm). For each, record from NSE or Screener:
   - Last traded price (LTP)
   - Market capitalisation
   - 52-week high and low
   - Today's traded volume
3. Open the **market depth / order book** for one of them and write down the best bid, the best
   ask, and the spread. Then do the same for a small-cap. Compare the spreads.
4. Write a 3-sentence answer: *"If I had ₹10,000 of imaginary money and had to buy one of these
   three, which would I pick and why?"* — Reasoning matters, the answer doesn't. Do not buy anything.

**Time:** ~45 minutes.

## 5. Checkpoint quiz

Answer without looking. Answers at the bottom.

1. You buy shares of Company X on the NSE from another investor. How much of your money reaches Company X?
2. Company Y announces a 1:1 bonus. You hold 100 shares worth ₹200 each. What do you hold after, and what is it worth?
3. Bid is ₹450.00, ask is ₹450.90. You place a market **buy** order. Roughly what price do you get?
4. Which is bigger: a company with a ₹3,000 share price, or one with a ₹30 share price?
5. What is the difference between NSDL/CDSL and your broker?
6. Nifty 50 rose 0.8% today. Does that mean most Indian stocks rose today?
7. What does SEBI do that an exchange does not?
8. You want to buy a small-cap but only if you get it at ₹95 or lower. Which order type?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. **Zero.** Secondary market trades transfer money between investors. Only the primary market (IPO/FPO/rights) funds the company.
2. 200 shares worth ~₹100 each = ₹20,000. **Same total value.**
3. Around **₹450.90** (the ask) or worse — a market buy lifts the offer.
4. **Unknowable from price alone.** You need market cap = price × share count.
5. NSDL/CDSL are the **depositories** that hold your shares; the broker is the **intermediary** that routes your orders and usually acts as your DP.
6. **No.** It means the 50 large-cap constituents, weighted by free float, collectively rose. Breadth can still be negative.
7. SEBI **regulates** — registers intermediaries, frames rules, investigates and penalises. The exchange **operates a market** and enforces its own listing/trading rules under SEBI's oversight.
8. A **limit** buy order at ₹95.

</details>

## 7. What you should NOT worry about yet

- Which stock to buy. (Not this week. Not for several weeks.)
- Futures, options, lot sizes, option Greeks.
- Intraday trading, margin, leverage, MIS/CNC product codes.
- Candlestick patterns and indicators.
- Taxation (STCG/LTCG/STT) — Week 7+, and only when you have real transactions.
- Which broker is "best". You don't need to open an account this week.
- Macro news, RBI policy commentary, FII/DII flows, global cues.
