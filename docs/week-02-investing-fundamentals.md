**Time:** 2–3 hours

## 1. Learning objectives

By the end of this week you should be able to:

1. Clearly separate **saving**, **investing**, **trading**, and **speculation**.
2. Compute compounding yourself and explain why time matters more than returns early on.
3. Explain **real return** (return after inflation) and why 6% in an FD can be a loss.
4. Match a **time horizon** to an appropriate **asset class**.
5. Explain what diversification does — and what it cannot do.
6. Read a mutual fund factsheet: NAV, expense ratio, category, benchmark, direct vs regular.
7. Build your first **mock portfolio** with a written rationale.

## 2. Concepts, explained simply

### 2.1 Saving vs investing vs trading

| | Saving | Investing | Trading |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Preserve money, stay liquid | Grow purchasing power over years | Profit from price moves over days/weeks |
| Horizon | Days–months | 5+ years | Minutes–months |
| Main risk | Inflation erodes it | Price falls; business fails | Rapid capital loss, costs |
| Skill needed | Discipline | Patience + basic analysis | Process, risk control, statistics |
| Where | Savings a/c, FD, liquid fund | Equity, index funds, bonds, real assets | Equity intraday/swing, F&O |

> **Speculation** → Taking a position mainly on a *price move* rather than on the underlying
> business's cash flows. → Buying an unprofitable company because "it will run". → It matters
> because speculation isn't inherently evil, but pretending it's investing is how people lose money
> without realising the risk they took.

Full breakdown: [Investing vs Trading vs Speculation vs Gambling](investing-vs-trading-vs-speculation.md)

### 2.2 Compounding

> **Compounding** → Earning returns on your past returns, not just on your original amount.
> → ₹1,00,000 at 12% for 10 years ≈ ₹3.1 lakh; for 20 years ≈ ₹9.6 lakh; for 30 years ≈ ₹29.9 lakh.
> → It matters because the last decade contributes more than the first two combined. Time is the
> ingredient you cannot buy later.

**Formula:** `FV = PV × (1 + r)^n`
- `PV` = amount today, `r` = annual return (as a decimal), `n` = years

**Rule of 72** → Years to double ≈ 72 ÷ return %.
- 12% → ~6 years to double. 8% → ~9 years. 6% → ~12 years.

> **CAGR (Compound Annual Growth Rate)** → The single smoothed annual rate that takes you from
> the start value to the end value. → ₹1L → ₹2L in 6 years = ~12.2% CAGR. → It matters because
> "my stock gave 60% returns" is meaningless without knowing over how long.

`CAGR = (End / Start)^(1/n) − 1`

⚠️ **Absolute return ≠ CAGR.** 60% over 6 years is ~8.1% CAGR — worse than it sounds.

### 2.3 Inflation and real return

> **Inflation** → The rate at which prices rise, i.e. the rate at which your money loses
> purchasing power. → In India, CPI inflation has generally run in the mid-single digits; RBI's
> flexible inflation-targeting mandate is **4% CPI with a ±2% band**. → It matters because it is
> the hurdle every investment must clear before it has done anything for you.

> **Real return** → Nominal return minus inflation. → An FD at 6.5% when inflation is 6% gives a
> **real return of ~0.5% before tax** — and after tax at 30% slab it's *negative*. → It matters
> because "safe" instruments can guarantee a slow, certain loss of purchasing power.

Check current CPI/WPI here: RBI — https://www.rbi.org.in/ · MoSPI — https://mospi.gov.in/

### 2.4 Risk vs return, and time horizon

> **Risk** → In practice, two things: (a) permanent loss of capital, and (b) volatility — how
> much the value swings. → Equity can fall 30–50% in a bad year; a liquid fund barely moves.
> → It matters because higher expected return is *compensation* for enduring risk, not a free gift.

**Volatility is not the same as risk of ruin.** For a 20-year horizon, a 40% drawdown is
survivable turbulence. For money you need in 8 months, it is a disaster.

| Money needed in… | Reasonable place for it | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 0–1 year | Savings account, liquid/overnight fund, short FD | Capital safety beats returns |
| 1–3 years | Short-duration debt funds, FDs | Equity can be down over 1–3 years |
| 3–5 years | Hybrid / conservative mix | Partial equity exposure |
| 5–10+ years | Diversified equity, index funds | Time smooths equity's bad years |

> **Emergency fund** → 6 months of expenses kept in cash-like instruments. → ₹40,000/month
> spending → ~₹2.4 lakh. → It matters because without one, the first emergency forces you to sell
> equity at the worst possible time. **This comes before any investing.**

### 2.5 Diversification and asset allocation

> **Diversification** → Spreading money across holdings that don't all fail together. → 15 stocks
> across 8 sectors instead of 3 stocks in one sector. → It matters because it removes the risk of
> being *wrong about one company*, at no cost in expected return.

**What diversification does NOT do:** protect you in a broad market crash. When the whole market
falls, correlations go to 1. Diversification handles *company-specific* risk, not *market* risk.

⚠️ **Diworsification:** owning 12 large-cap mutual funds is not diversification — they all hold
the same 40 stocks. You've added cost and complexity, not safety.

> **Asset allocation** → How you split money across asset classes (equity / debt / gold / cash).
> → 70% equity, 20% debt, 10% gold. → It matters because research consistently shows allocation
> drives most of the variation in a portfolio's outcome — far more than which specific stock you picked.

> **Rebalancing** → Periodically restoring your target mix by selling what grew and buying what
> lagged. → Equity grew to 80% of a 70/30 target → sell 10 points, move it to debt. → It matters
> because it mechanically forces "sell high, buy low" without requiring a forecast.

### 2.6 Asset classes

| Asset class | What it is | Typical role |
|---|---|---|
| **Equity (stocks)** | Ownership in businesses | Long-term growth; high volatility |
| **Bonds / debt** | You lend; issuer pays interest | Stability, income; interest-rate & credit risk |
| **Mutual funds** | Pooled money managed by an AMC | Diversified access, professional management |
| **Index funds / ETFs** | Funds that simply replicate an index | Low cost, no fund-manager risk |
| **Gold** | Store of value, ₹-hedge | Diversifier; produces no cash flow |
| **Real estate** | Property | Illiquid, large ticket size |

> **Mutual fund** → A pooled vehicle where many investors' money is managed together by an AMC
> under SEBI regulation. → An index fund tracking the Nifty 50 buys all 50 stocks in index weights.
> → It matters because it gives a beginner instant diversification for a few hundred rupees.

> **NAV (Net Asset Value)** → Per-unit value of the fund = (assets − liabilities) ÷ units.
> → ₹52.40 per unit. → It matters because — importantly — **a low NAV is not "cheap"**. A ₹10 NAV
> fund is not better value than a ₹500 NAV fund. Only the *percentage change* matters.

> **Expense ratio (TER)** → Annual fee charged by the fund, as % of assets, already deducted from
> NAV. → 0.20% for an index fund vs 1.75% for an active fund. → It matters because a 1.5%
> difference compounds into a very large gap over 20 years. Fees are the one variable you control.

> **Direct vs Regular plan** → Direct = you buy from the AMC, no distributor commission.
> Regular = commission is embedded in a higher expense ratio. → Same portfolio, different TER.
> → It matters because over decades, Direct plans of the same scheme structurally outperform
> Regular ones by roughly the commission.

> **ETF (Exchange Traded Fund)** → A fund whose units trade on the exchange like a share.
> → A Nifty 50 ETF. → It matters because you need a demat account and must watch the
> bid-ask spread and premium/discount to iNAV; an index *fund* avoids that hassle.

> **Index fund** → A fund that mechanically replicates an index rather than trying to beat it.
> → It matters because most active large-cap funds fail to beat their benchmark over long periods
> after fees. Check the evidence yourself in the **SPIVA India scorecard** (below).

> **Tracking error / tracking difference** → How much an index fund's return deviates from the
> index. → It matters because for index funds, low cost + low tracking error is basically the
> entire selection criterion.

### 2.7 SIP

> **SIP (Systematic Investment Plan)** → Investing a fixed amount at fixed intervals, usually
> monthly. → ₹5,000 into an index fund on the 5th of every month. → It matters because it
> automates the decision, removes timing anxiety, and buys more units when prices are low
> (**rupee cost averaging**).

**Honest caveats:**
- SIP is a *method of investing*, not an asset class or a product.
- A SIP does **not** protect you from loss. In a falling market, your SIP value falls too.
- Lump sum invested early can beat SIP mathematically — SIP wins on *behaviour*, which is
  usually the binding constraint.
- **Step-up SIP** — increasing the amount each year with your income — is one of the highest
  impact things a young investor can do.

### 2.8 Dividends

> **Dividend** → Cash a company distributes out of profits to shareholders. → ₹12 per share on a
> ₹600 share = **2% dividend yield**. → It matters because it's a real cash return, but a high
> yield can also signal a company with no growth opportunities to reinvest in.

`Dividend yield = Annual dividend per share ÷ Share price`

⚠️ Dividends are not free money — the share price adjusts downward on the ex-date. In India,
dividends are **taxable in the investor's hands at their slab rate**, with TDS above a threshold.
(Verify current rules; tax law changes.)

> **Total return** → Price change **plus** dividends. → It matters because comparing a
> dividend-paying stock's price chart to a non-payer's is an apples-to-oranges comparison.
> Nifty has both a "Price Return" (TRI vs PR) and a **Total Return Index (TRI)** version — funds
> must benchmark against **TRI**.

## 4. Practical exercises

### Exercise A — Compounding maths by hand (20 min)
Worksheet: [Compounding & SIP](exercises/02-compounding-and-sip.md)

1. Compute the future value of ₹1,00,000 at 12% for 10, 20, and 30 years. Note the *shape*.
2. Compute the future value of a ₹5,000/month SIP at 12% for 10, 20, and 30 years.
   Then compute how much of that final amount was **your contribution** vs **growth**.
3. Redo (2) with a **10% step-up each year**. Compare.
4. Compute the **real return** of an FD at 7% when inflation is 5.5% and you're in the 30% tax slab.
5. A fund returned 96% over 8 years. What's the CAGR? Does it still sound impressive?

### Exercise B — Read a real mutual fund factsheet (20 min)
Pick **one Nifty 50 index fund** and **one active large-cap fund**. Record:
- Expense ratio (Direct plan) for each
- Benchmark used (is it TRI?)
- 5-year and 10-year returns vs benchmark
- AUM and fund category
- For the index fund: tracking error

Then answer: *over 20 years, roughly how much does the TER difference cost on ₹10 lakh?*

### Exercise C — Build your mock portfolio (25 min)
Worksheet: [Mock portfolio tracker](exercises/mock-portfolio.md)

Allocate an imaginary **₹5,00,000**:
- Decide an asset allocation first (equity / debt / gold / cash) and write **why**.
- Then fill it with specific instruments (index fund, a few large caps, a debt fund, gold ETF).
- Write **one sentence of rationale per holding** and a **target holding period**.
- Record the date and the price/NAV of everything.

You will revisit this portfolio in Weeks 3–6 and again after 3 months. **No real money.**

## 5. Checkpoint quiz

1. Fund A has NAV ₹15, Fund B has NAV ₹450. Which is cheaper?
2. Your FD pays 7%, inflation is 6%, and you're taxed at 30% on the interest. What's your real return?
3. What's the CAGR if ₹1,00,000 becomes ₹2,00,000 in 6 years?
4. You own 10 different large-cap mutual funds. Are you well diversified?
5. Why does a share price typically fall on the ex-dividend date?
6. You need ₹6 lakh in 18 months for a specific purpose. Should it be in equity?
7. What is the single biggest advantage of a Direct plan over a Regular plan?
8. Does a SIP protect you from losing money?

<details class="success" markdown="1">
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. **Neither.** NAV level is irrelevant — it reflects the fund's history, not its value. Compare TER, category, and returns.
2. Post-tax return = 7% × (1 − 0.30) = **4.9%**. Real return = 4.9% − 6% = **−1.1%**. You lost purchasing power.
3. (2)^(1/6) − 1 ≈ **12.2%**.
4. **No.** Large-cap funds hold heavily overlapping portfolios. You have one exposure with ten sets of paperwork.
5. Because the company is paying out cash it previously held — the business is literally worth that much less per share afterwards.
6. **No.** 18 months is far too short for equity. Use a liquid/short-duration debt product or an FD.
7. No embedded distributor commission → **lower expense ratio** → higher compounded return for the same portfolio.
8. **No.** It reduces timing risk and enforces discipline. The underlying asset can still fall.

</details>

## 7. What you should NOT worry about yet

- Picking "the best mutual fund". Any low-cost broad index fund is a fine placeholder for learning.
- Star ratings, "top 10 funds" lists, and past-1-year return tables.
- ELSS/tax-saving optimisation, NPS vs PPF debates.
- International funds, thematic/sectoral funds, smart-beta, factor investing.
- Reading balance sheets — that's next week.
- Any chart or indicator.
