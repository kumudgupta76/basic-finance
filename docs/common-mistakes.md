# Common Beginner Mistakes

Ordered roughly by how much damage they cause.

---

## Tier 1 — Account-destroying

### 1. Trading F&O early
Leverage plus a deadline plus costs. SEBI's own published studies on individual traders in the
equity derivatives segment have repeatedly found the large majority lose money, with losses
concentrated among the youngest and smallest participants.
**Fix:** don't touch derivatives for at least a year, and only then with a written system and money
you can lose entirely.

### 2. Trading without a stop loss
One unbounded loss can erase dozens of disciplined wins.
**Fix:** the stop is decided before entry, placed where your idea is invalidated, and never widened.

### 3. Using leverage / MTF as a beginner
At 5×, a 20% adverse move is 100% of your capital. Leverage doesn't improve your odds of being
right, only the cost of being wrong.
**Fix:** zero leverage. Not "a little". Zero.

### 4. Revenge trading
Losing, then immediately taking a bigger unplanned position to get it back. This is how a bad day
becomes a wiped-out account.
**Fix:** hard circuit breaker — two consecutive losses, stop for the day.

### 5. Position sizing backwards
Deciding "I'll buy 100 shares" and then inventing a stop. This makes your risk arbitrary.
**Fix:** `Size = (Capital × Risk%) ÷ (Entry − Stop)`. The stop determines the size, always.

### 6. Investing money you'll need soon
Equity money that has to be withdrawn in 12 months will, sooner or later, be withdrawn at the worst
possible time.
**Fix:** match horizon to asset class. Under 3 years → not equity.

### 7. Skipping the emergency fund and insurance
Without them, the first medical bill or job loss forces you to liquidate at the bottom.
**Fix:** 6 months of expenses in cash-like instruments, plus health and term cover, before investing.

## Tier 2 — Steadily expensive

### 8. Following tips
Telegram groups, WhatsApp forwards, YouTube "targets", "sure-shot calls". You never learn why, so
you never know when to exit. Many are outright pump-and-dump operations, and SEBI regularly acts
against unregistered advisers.
**Fix:** if you can't explain the thesis yourself, you can't hold the position.

### 9. Buying penny stocks and unknown micro-caps
Low price feels like low risk. It isn't. These are often illiquid, poorly governed, and easily
manipulated — and you may be unable to exit at any price when circuits hit.
**Fix:** start with liquid large caps or an index fund.

### 10. Confusing a low share price with "cheap"
A ₹8 stock is not cheaper than a ₹4,000 stock. Only market cap relative to earnings/assets means
anything.
**Fix:** always think in market cap and multiples.

### 11. Thinking a low NAV mutual fund is cheaper
NAV level is an artefact of the fund's history. It has zero bearing on future returns.
**Fix:** compare expense ratio, category, benchmark, and consistency.

### 12. Buying Regular plans instead of Direct
You pay a commission every year, forever, embedded in the expense ratio, and it compounds against you.
**Fix:** Direct plans, unless you're knowingly paying for advice you value.

### 13. Owning 12 mutual funds and calling it diversification
Overlapping large-cap funds hold the same stocks. You've added paperwork, not safety.
**Fix:** 1–3 funds is plenty for most people. Check portfolio overlap.

### 14. Ignoring costs
Brokerage, STT, exchange fees, GST, stamp duty, and slippage. Frequent trading can convert a
marginally positive strategy into a reliably negative one.
**Fix:** model total round-trip cost before evaluating any strategy.

### 15. Chasing last year's best performer
Fund and sector leadership rotates. Buying what just did well is systematically buying high.
**Fix:** judge on process and cost, not on the trailing one-year table.

### 16. Ignoring taxes
Selling a week before long-term treatment kicks in, or churning a portfolio into a large short-term
tax bill.
**Fix:** understand STCG/LTCG rules before you sell, not after.

## Tier 3 — Analytical errors

### 17. Using standalone instead of consolidated statements
Problems get parked in subsidiaries. Standalone hides them.
**Fix:** always consolidated.

### 18. Trusting profit and ignoring cash flow
Profit is an accounting opinion; cash is a fact. Rising profit with flat or negative CFO is the
classic pre-blowup signature.
**Fix:** compare CFO to net profit over 5–10 years, every time.

### 19. Treating EBITDA as cash flow
EBITDA excludes interest, tax, capex, and working capital — most of what actually consumes cash.
**Fix:** look at FCF (CFO − capex).

### 20. Comparing P/E across sectors
An IT firm at 26× and a bank at 11× tells you nothing. Cyclicals are cheapest-looking at peak earnings.
**Fix:** compare like with like, and use P/B for financials.

### 21. Being impressed by ROE without checking leverage
Debt shrinks the equity base and inflates ROE while making the company fragile.
**Fix:** read ROE next to ROCE and debt/equity.

### 22. Not reading the notes
Contingent liabilities, related-party transactions, and auditor qualifications live in the notes,
and they're where the actual risks are disclosed.
**Fix:** read the auditor's report and the related-party note every time.

### 23. Anchoring to a single year
One good year proves nothing. Trends over 5–10 years and consistency are what matter.
**Fix:** always look at the multi-year series.

## Tier 4 — Technical / trading errors

### 24. Treating RSI > 70 as a sell signal
In a strong trend RSI stays overbought for weeks. Shorting on this alone is a well-known way to lose.
**Fix:** use RSI for divergence and trend-strength context, not for calling tops.

### 25. Stacking ten indicators
All derived from the same price series. It's the illusion of confirmation.
**Fix:** two indicators maximum, plus price structure.

### 26. Memorising candlestick patterns without context
The same hammer means different things at support and in the middle of a range.
**Fix:** level and volume first, pattern second.

### 27. Chasing breakouts without volume or a retest
False breakouts are extremely common and specifically designed by market structure to trap chasers.
**Fix:** require a close beyond the level plus above-average volume, or wait for the retest.

### 28. Trading intraday as a beginner
Highest costs, highest noise, highest emotional load, competing against algorithms.
**Fix:** start with daily/weekly charts and swing or positional horizons — or don't trade at all.

### 29. Believing backtests and hindsight charts
Everything is obvious looking left. Real time is different.
**Fix:** use bar replay; make decisions before revealing the next candle.

## Tier 5 — Behavioural

### 30. FOMO buying
Buying because it's already up 20% and everyone's talking about it — the worst price and the widest stop.
**Fix:** not in the plan, not a trade. A missed opportunity costs zero.

### 31. Averaging down mechanically
Adding to a loser only because you're already in it is doubling down on being wrong.
**Fix:** only add on a *fresh, independent* thesis — and never beyond your position limit.

### 32. Selling winners early, holding losers forever
The disposition effect. It inverts your risk/reward and guarantees long-run losses.
**Fix:** pre-set stops and targets; act mechanically.

### 33. Checking the portfolio ten times a day
More checking → more reacting → more trading → more costs and worse decisions.
**Fix:** a scheduled weekly review. Turn off price alerts.

### 34. Judging decisions by outcome instead of process
A rule-breaking trade that made money teaches you the most expensive lesson possible.
**Fix:** grade every trade on plan compliance, separately from P&L.

### 35. Only journalling the wins
Selective memory turns luck into perceived skill.
**Fix:** log every trade, especially the embarrassing ones. Re-read monthly.

### 36. Overconfidence after a good streak
Sizing up after wins, right before the mean reverts.
**Fix:** size comes from rules, never from mood or recent results.

### 37. Expecting a monthly salary from the market
Returns are lumpy and unpredictable. Needing a fixed monthly outcome forces bad trades.
**Fix:** trading income, if ever, comes after years and a separate income source.

## Tier 6 — Safety and fraud

### 38. Dealing with unregistered entities
Anyone offering "guaranteed returns", "assured monthly income from the market", or asking you to
transfer funds to a personal account is a fraud.
**Fix:** verify registration at https://www.sebi.gov.in/intermediaries.html . Guaranteed equity
returns do not exist.

### 39. Sharing trading credentials or letting someone "manage" your account
Handing over login details is how accounts get emptied, and you'll have little recourse.
**Fix:** never share credentials. Use only regulated, documented arrangements.

### 40. Not registering nominees
A demat account without a nominee creates years of difficulty for your family.
**Fix:** add nominees now. It takes five minutes.

### 41. Not knowing how to complain
**Fix:** SEBI SCORES — https://scores.sebi.gov.in/ — plus the exchange's investor grievance cell and
the Online Dispute Resolution portal.

---

## The one-line summary

> Most beginners lose money not because they picked the wrong stock, but because they took
> **too much size**, with **too much leverage**, on **too little conviction**, with **no exit plan**,
> using **money they needed** — and then doubled down when it went wrong.

Avoid those six things and you've avoided most of the damage available in markets.
